Why do penguins walk away from the colony?

What scientists know about Adélie penguins that wander inland like the nihilist penguin: disorientation, bad weather, how rare it is and what is unknown.

Updated 2026-10-11

The best scientific explanation for penguins like the "nihilist penguin" is disorientation. Adélie penguins usually find their way using the sun and landmarks. If the sun is hidden or the landscape is featureless, an individual can lose its bearings and walk the wrong way, sometimes for hundreds of kilometres. Such walks are rare. Researchers describe them as maladaptive: they don't help the bird, and they usually end badly. Nobody has studied enough cases to say exactly why a particular bird does it, and there is no evidence of anything like despair or choice.

What the penguin in the film was

The bird in Werner Herzog's Encounters at the End of the World was an Adélie penguin at a colony near McMurdo Station. When Herzog asked penguin scientist David Ainley whether penguins can go insane, Ainley said he had never seen one "bashing its head against a rock". He added that penguins do get disoriented and can end up far from the ocean, as reported by Little White Lies and Distractify. Ainley's word for it, disoriented, is still what researchers say today. More on the scene is on the original clip page.

Why walking inland makes no sense for an Adélie

According to the Australian Antarctic Program:

  • Adélies are medium-sized penguins, 3–6 kg and about 70 cm tall, that breed on exposed rock along the Antarctic coast and on small islands.
  • They nest on land from October to February. Chicks hatch in December and fledge at 7–9 weeks.
  • All their food comes from the sea: fish, amphipods and krill. Breeding adults make trips of 5–120 km offshore that last up to about three days.
  • When sea ice still blocks the coast, they may walk more than 50 km across it to reach open water.

Walking long distances is normal for an Adélie, as long as it's heading towards the sea. Inland, the Antarctic interior has no food, so a bird walking towards the mountains is walking away from everything it needs.

The strongest evidence: a 2026 sighting 200 km from the sea

A short research note published in the journal Polar Research on 6 April 2026 is the clearest recent scientific account. Its authors are Sébastien Descamps, Anca Cristea and Geir Ove Aspnes of the Norwegian Polar Institute.

The authors suggest the bad weather may have played a part. Their main explanation is disorientation in featureless terrain, especially in fog or under cloud.

Older records

The paper reviews the few earlier reports:

  • Tracks about 300 km inland recorded in 1958 (Sladen and Ostenso, 1960).
  • A track near Troll Airfield in 2002 that the authors think was probably an Adélie.
  • Accounts from British Antarctic expeditions suggesting occasional inland wandering, though how often it happens and the details are unknown.

How Adélies normally navigate

The research note summarises displacement experiments from the 1960s (Emlen and Penney, 1964; Penney and Emlen, 1967). Researchers moved Adélies away from their colonies and watched what they did:

  • Birds could walk up to about 60 km a day.
  • Birds moved as far as 300 km from their colony could still get home.
  • When the sun was hidden by cloud and the birds were far from familiar landmarks, they could lose their sense of direction.

This shows Adélies are capable navigators whose system depends on conditions. With a clear sky they can home over long distances. In flat white terrain under cloud, the same bird can head confidently in the wrong direction. This might also explain the detail in Herzog's film where a bird carried back to the colony sets off the same way again, though that is our reading rather than a tested finding.

Other explanations you will see

News explainers from January 2026 list more possible causes:

  • Illness or neurological problems. The Business Standard and an explainer carried by AOL both mention these.
  • Extreme stress during the breeding season, according to The Business Standard.
  • Navigational error, the same idea as disorientation.

These are reasonable possibilities, but the articles name no studies for them, and we did not find peer-reviewed work linking a particular illness to inland wandering in Adélies. Treat them as hypotheses, not findings.

Some social media posts say the penguin was heartbroken after losing a mate. No source we found supports this. It is a story people have attached to the clip.

What is still uncertain

  • Why one bird and not others. The Polar Research authors say the mechanisms behind these inland movements remain largely unknown.
  • How common it is. The evidence is a handful of sightings and tracks over almost 70 years. Most wanderers in the interior would never be seen.
  • What happened to Herzog's penguin. Nobody filmed or recorded its fate. Survival was very unlikely, but no record exists (see the clip page).
  • Whether the scene was what it looked like. A documentary shows what the editor chose. The footage alone doesn't tell us how long the bird was watched.

The short answer

Storyboard18's January 2026 explainer put it simply: scientists see the behaviour as disorientation that is almost always fatal. The penguin isn't making a philosophical choice. Its navigation has failed, probably in poor visibility, and it keeps walking on the wrong heading. The meme's meanings, from burnout to "be that penguin", come from the people who post it, as the meaning page explains. For the full story of the meme, see the nihilist penguin hub.
