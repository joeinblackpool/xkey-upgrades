What does jirai-kei mean? Landmine fashion explained

Jirai-kei means "landmine type": a dark, frilly Japanese style. What the word implies, where it came from, and how it differs from ryōsan-gata.

Jirai-kei (地雷系) means "landmine type" in Japanese. It began as online slang for a woman who seems sweet but can "go off" without warning in a relationship, and it is now the name of a fashion style: frilly, girly clothes in black, white and dusky pink, worn with makeup that makes the eyes look puffy, as if from crying. Its lighter sister style, ryōsan-gata (量産型, "mass-produced type"), uses the same frills and bows in soft pink and beige.

The word itself

"Jirai": the landmine

The fashion name comes from jirai onna, "landmine woman", a phrase that first circulated on anonymous Japanese internet forums. It described someone who comes across as gentle but behaves in an emotionally unstable or unpredictable way once you get close. The image is a mine: harmless-looking until you step on it.

So the word started as an insult. Tokyo Weekender describes it as a put-down for women seen as emotionally volatile, and Yokogao Magazine goes further, calling the label misogynistic because it was mostly pinned on women. Rolling Stone India gives the same meaning in neutral terms: someone easily triggered and prone to outbursts.

"Kei": the style

Kei (系) means "type", which is why the sources translate jirai-kei as "landmine type". In fashion use it works like "style", so jirai-kei reads as "landmine-type style".

How an insult became a fashion label

Tokyo Weekender credits a pandemic-era makeup challenge by Japanese YouTuber Surero with making the look widely known: pale foundation, heavy rosy blush and lines under the eyes that suggested fragility. Young women then adopted the word for themselves and their clothes. Yokogao Magazine also puts the mainstream breakthrough in the 2020s, helped by a YouTuber who popularised the makeup.

What jirai-kei looks like

The core elements, drawn from Wikipedia, Tokyo Weekender and Rolling Stone India:

  • Colours: black, white and dusky pink.
  • Clothes: lace blouses with puff sleeves, frilled or high-waisted skirts, dresses and two-piece set-ups, plaid dresses, oversized cardigans.
  • Accessories: chokers, heart-buckle belts, layered necklaces, ribbons, silver jewellery and motifs such as crosses, hearts and safety pins. A studded MCM mini backpack is often mentioned as a near-staple, as are Vivienne Westwood necklaces.
  • Shoes: chunky-heeled Mary Janes or platform boots.
  • Hair: long twin tails, often with ribbons, or soft waves.
  • Makeup: pale base, red or pink colour under the lower lashes (the "jirai line"), drooping eyeliner and large coloured contact lenses.
  • Mascot: Sanrio's Kuromi.

Rolling Stone India also describes variations: "subcul jirai", with oversized tops and baggy socks or leg warmers, and jirai danshi, a version for boys with oversized shirts, baggy trousers and jackets.

What does ryōsan-gata mean?

Ryōsan-gata is a factory term for a mass-produced model. Applied to people, it describes someone who follows trends so closely that they look like everyone else following the same trends. FUN! JAPAN explains the name this way: wearers end up resembling others with the same fashion choices.

The fashion version is tied to idol fandom. Ryōsan-gata wotaku are female fans of male idol groups who dress alike for concerts and fan events. Japanese shops tag this kind of clothing as sansen-fuku (参戦服, roughly "event outfit") alongside ryōsan-gata and jirai-kei, as on Secret Honey's Rakuten store.

The look is neat and sweet: tops and skirts with bows, pearls or heart details in soft pink, beige and white, with natural makeup in pink tones and glitter around the eyes. My Melody and Cinnamoroll are the Sanrio characters most linked with it. Wikipedia notes critics have called it a simplified Lolita style.

Academics have taken an interest too. A 2024 conference paper by researchers at University College London and Ohio State University argues that ryōsan-gata's lack of authenticity, the fact that it is openly about imitation, is its defining feature rather than a flaw.

The two overlap so much that Japanese retailers often sell them together. A June 2026 press release for a joint sale between the companies behind DearMyLove and ROJITA called the genre "ryōsan-gata jirai-kei" fashion and summed it up as cute but "a little bit dangerous".

The connotations to know about

Mental health language

Because the word means "unstable woman", the fashion has always carried mental-health overtones. Yokogao Magazine notes that wearers were also tagged menhera, slang associated with self-harm and obsessive love. Tokyo Weekender connects jirai-kei with yami kawaii ("sick cute"), which uses pills, syringes and bandages as motifs. Both sources say plainly that wearing the clothes does not mean someone is struggling.

Kabukichō and the Tōyoko kids

Jirai-kei is first associated with Kabukichō, Shinjuku's nightlife district. The Tōyoko kids, young people who gather by the Shinjuku Toho Building there, some of them runaways, often dress in the style. Reports link the area to host-club exploitation, substance abuse and underage sex work, and that has shaped how some people in Japan see the look.

Outside Japan, Tokyo Weekender says, many fans take only the aesthetic. That is a fair way to wear it, but it is worth knowing why some Japanese readers may react to the word.
