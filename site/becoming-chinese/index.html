Becoming Chinese trend: what it is and how to try it

The 2026 TikTok trend of adopting everyday Chinese habits: where it started, the main habits, what it costs to try and the criticism.

"Becoming Chinese" is a social media trend, mostly on TikTok and Instagram, in which people, many of them young Americans with no Chinese heritage, post videos of themselves picking up everyday habits associated with Chinese homes: drinking hot water instead of iced drinks, wearing slippers indoors, eating congee and soup, going to bed earlier and trying practices such as tai chi or gua sha. It took off in January 2026. Some of it is a deliberate joke, some of it is sincere, and a lot of it is both. This guide explains where it came from, what people actually do, what it costs to try, and why some Chinese and Chinese-diaspora writers have mixed feelings about it.

What the trend is

Participants describe themselves as being on a "becoming Chinese" journey, sometimes posting day counts, and swap tips about habits they have adopted. The trend is also called Chinamaxxing, and a version aimed at women uses the tag #chinesebaddie. NBC News described it in February 2026 as an intentionally absurd joke that also shines a light on traditional Chinese wellness habits. The Conversation's analysis, republished by Southern Cross University, called it part parody and part politics, and noted that most of the humorous videos are not mocking Chinese people.

A common caption is "you met me at a very Chinese time in my life", a remix of a line from the 1999 film Fight Club. For the meaning and the vocabulary behind it, see what does becoming Chinese mean.

Why interest is rising

The clearest evidence is reach. The South China Morning Post and GoEast Mandarin both report that videos showing these habits passed 20 million views by February 2026, and the trend was covered by NBC News, Complex, the South China Morning Post, The Conversation, Bloomberg and AP within a few months. It also spilled into shopping: Complex listed an Adidas track jacket with Chinese knot buttons among the most hyped items of 2026, saying it was reselling for double its $150 retail price before a wider release.

Commentators offer several explanations, which are interpretations rather than measured facts:

  • Burnout. The Conversation links the appeal of rest, warmth and moderation to fatigue with hustle culture and economic insecurity.
  • The RedNote moment. NBC and GoEast both point to January 2025, when many American TikTok users moved to the Chinese app Xiaohongshu (RedNote) during the threatened TikTok ban and talked directly with Chinese users.
  • Shifting views of China. NBC cites an October 2025 Chicago Council on Global Affairs poll in which 53% of Americans favoured cooperation and engagement with China, up from 40% in 2024.
  • Soft power. Travel vlogs, Labubu toys and Chinese drinks chains have raised China's cultural profile, according to GoEast and Wikipedia's summary of BBC reporting.

Know Your Meme describes the trend as having peaked in early 2026, so treat it as an established meme rather than something still accelerating.

The main habits

Most videos draw on a handful of everyday practices, many rooted in the Chinese idea of 养生 (yǎngshēng), "nourishing life":

  • Drinking hot or warm water and avoiding iced drinks
  • Wearing slippers indoors so your feet stay warm
  • Eating congee (rice porridge) and soups instead of salads
  • Boiled apple water with red dates and goji berries
  • Going to bed earlier
  • Morning tai chi or Baduanjin qigong
  • Foot soaks before bed
  • Gua sha, cupping or acupuncture

The background behind each one, and what health bodies do and do not say about them, is in becoming Chinese habits explained.

Who is joining in

The Conversation and GoEast describe the participants as mostly Gen Z users in the United States, with GoEast adding Europe. Many are not of Chinese heritage. Chinese-diaspora creators such as Sherry Zhu, Angela Shan Hu and Kaila Wenn have played along, and NBC credits them with taking it mainstream. NBC also notes that comedian Jimmy O. Yang joined a related meme.

Downsides and criticism

The trend has drawn criticism, mainly from Chinese and Asian-diaspora writers:

  • Hurt and scepticism. NBC spoke to Chinese Americans who remember being bullied over their food or habits, and anti-Asian blame during COVID-19. One creator, Jennifer Lee, worried it could feel like "we're the laughingstock again".
  • Who gets credit. PureWow editor Marissa Wu, who is Chinese American, wrote that she liked the curiosity but questioned who profits from "being Chinese" and whether acceptance should depend on non-Asian approval.
  • Flattening the culture. An LBB Online piece by Abigail Goh of We Are Social argued that algorithms reward simple, confident narration, turning traditional Chinese medicine into a lifestyle accessory and stripping context from items such as the Tang jacket.
  • Stereotypes. Wikipedia summarises further criticism in Wired, Time and from a RAND scholar, including that some portrayals have racist or sarcastic undertones.

Supporters quoted by NBC, such as Angela Shan Hu, argue the trend is appreciation rather than appropriation because participants openly credit the habits' Chinese origins.

There are practical cautions too. Some videos promote treatments such as cupping or acupuncture, which carry real risks for some people. This page is general information, not medical advice; check with your GP or pharmacist before trying anything that could affect a health condition or medicine you take.

How to get started

  1. Start with the free habits. Swap one iced drink a day for warm water or tea, and move your bedtime earlier.
  2. Learn the why. Read about 养生 and the ideas of warmth and balance behind the habits rather than treating them as a checklist.
  3. Cook one dish. Plain congee needs only rice and water.
  4. Follow Chinese creators. Many of the most-watched videos come from Chinese and Chinese-diaspora creators who explain their own families' habits.
  5. Spend only when you need to. A flask or slippers are the only items most people buy.
