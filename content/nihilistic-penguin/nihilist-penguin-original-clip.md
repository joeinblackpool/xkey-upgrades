The nihilist penguin clip comes from *Encounters at the End of the World*, a 2007 documentary by Werner Herzog about the people who live and work at McMurdo Station, the main US research base in Antarctica. Near the end of the film, Herzog visits a nearby penguin colony and talks to marine ecologist David Ainley. He then follows one Adélie penguin that walks away from the sea and heads inland towards mountains he says are about 70 km away. The film premiered in September 2007. When we checked in October 2026, it was not on any US subscription streaming service and was available to buy on disc. Details are below.

The film is not a nature documentary in the *March of the Penguins* sense. Herzog spends most of it interviewing scientists, support staff and drifters at McMurdo and filming places such as the South Pole and the volcano Mount Erebus. In a 2017 essay for the UK film magazine Little White Lies, Tim Cooke argued that the penguin scene is a deliberate counterpoint to sentimental penguin films. In his reading, the film is about how humans respond to nature, not about penguins.

## The scene

The penguin sequence comes about an hour and 13 minutes in, according to USA Today. Little White Lies places it at Cape Royds, an Adélie colony near McMurdo. It runs in roughly three parts.

### 1. The interview with David Ainley

Herzog introduces Ainley, the penguin scientist working at the colony. Little White Lies says Herzog describes him in the narration as a man who no longer has much interest in conversation with humans. Ainley explains that the colony has had a good winter, and that females have left the males to tend the eggs.

Herzog then asks him whether penguins can go insane. Distractify transcribes Ainley's answer as:

> "Well, I've never seen a penguin bashing its head against a rock."

He adds that penguins do get disoriented, and can end up in places far from the ocean where they shouldn't be.

### 2. The walk

Herzog's narration takes over. Most of the colony is heading towards the open water to feed. One bird stops at the edge of the group, stands still, then turns and walks inland. Herzog says it is heading straight for the mountains about 70 km away. He says that even if the bird were caught and carried back to the colony, it would immediately head back towards the mountains. Then he asks the question that became the meme's caption: "But why?"

Some 2026 news reports attribute the "it would turn round again" point to Ainley rather than Herzog. In the film, it is Herzog's narration that says it. USA Today notes that it was something an ecologist had told him.

### 3. The second penguin

Little White Lies and Distractify both note that the sequence also shows another penguin, which Herzog says is about 80 km off course, sliding on its belly in the wrong direction. Herzog presents both birds as heading towards certain death.

## Did the penguin die?

Probably, but nobody recorded it. Herzog presents the walk as a death march. UNB's January 2026 piece says experts "confirmed" the bird died after walking about 70 km, but it names no expert and cites no record. A separate explainer carried by AOL says there is no confirmed record of what happened after the bird left the frame, and we found nothing to contradict that. Adélie penguins find all their food at sea, according to the Australian Antarctic Program, so a long walk inland would be very hard to survive. The [science page](/nihilistic-penguin/why-do-penguins-walk-away/) covers what researchers know about these walks.

## How the clip got online

According to Know Your Meme:

- **3 November 2008.** The earliest known YouTube upload, titled "Deranged Penguin".
- **21 August 2015.** A YouTube upload titled "Nihilist Penguin" becomes the best-known early version.
- **2 November 2020.** A full upload of the film appears on a YouTube channel called Antarctic Digital Heritage. USA Today also mentions it.
- **January 2026.** TikTok edits make it a mass meme; see the [hub page](/nihilistic-penguin/) for the timeline.

We could not confirm whether the 2020 upload is authorised by the rights holders, or whether it is still online.

## How to watch it legally

Availability changes often, and it differs between the UK and the US. Check a listings service such as JustWatch for your country before paying.

### United States

When we checked in October 2026, JustWatch's US page for the film listed:

- **Subscription streaming:** none
- **Rent:** none listed
- **Buy:** DVD from $9.43 and Blu-ray from $12.97

Apple TV has a US storefront page for the film, crediting Image Entertainment and Discovery Channel, but it showed no buy or rent price when we opened it. That may mean it isn't currently offered there, or the page may simply not have loaded prices for us.

### United Kingdom

The film had a UK cinema and home release through Revolver Entertainment in 2009. We could not open a reliable UK listing to confirm current streaming, rental or purchase options. That doesn't mean it's unavailable, only that we couldn't verify it. UK readers should check JustWatch UK, the major digital stores and second-hand DVD sellers. Public libraries are also worth asking.

### Short clips

Short clips of the scene appear on YouTube and TikTok. Many appear to be reposts rather than official uploads, so they can disappear at any time. Watching the whole film gives you the Ainley interview and the rest of Herzog's Antarctica, which the 30-second meme version leaves out.
